01 — The challenge

Fashion ideas move fast. Fashion production doesn’t.

For many designers, the creative part is the easy part. The difficult part starts when an idea has to become something real.

A garment needs to be visualised, constructed, measured, documented and communicated to manufacturers. That often means moving between sketches, Illustrator files, spreadsheets, 3D tools, tech packs and physical samples.

Existing 3D tools are powerful, but they’re built for trained specialists. For everyone else, the learning curve is high.

Fabra’s opportunity was to make 3D fashion design accessible in the browser, without reducing it to a lightweight mockup tool.

The challenge wasn’t just making 3D easier. It was making it useful enough to become part of a real design-to-production workflow.

How do you make professional-grade 3D fashion design dramatically easier to use, without sacrificing the depth professional designers need?

02 — Scope

Solo in-house designer

I joined Fabra as its only designer, with a brief that ran wider than the canvas.

Own the user experience as the product moved from early access to public beta. Balance first-time creators against returning power users. Deliver everything from wireframes to high-fidelity designs, and build the reusable UI system underneath them.

Run the research programme across Fabra Friends, the waitlist and early adopters. Turn what we learned into what we built.

Design the full growth funnel: video → landing page → design tool → share → sign-up. And own the path from concept to production output — tech packs, exports, downloadable assets.

Alongside that, work directly with the CPO to shape the roadmap from research and behavioural data.

By launch, that brief had become a set of things that exist because I was in the room: a design principle the whole product runs on, a public beta scoped from my research rather than a feature wishlist, a performance fix that moved ahead of the roadmap because I kept putting users in front of the team, and a funnel converting visitors to designers at 7.6%.

03 — The early signal

People could start using Fabra quickly. The problem was what happened next.

In private beta, I worked closely with fashion designers, founders, print designers and creative professionals to understand how Fabra fitted into their existing workflows.

I ran 20 usability sessions with Gloria, our CPO, over Zoom — designers joined from all over the world. Each call followed a 30/70 structure: a third of the time watching people work through scenario-based tasks in Fabra, the rest understanding how they actually worked day to day, with or without us.

I synthesised the findings by persona — solo founder, team-based studio, print designer — and delivered the public beta change list in Notion: what to fix, what to add, what to leave alone. That document became the team’s brief for public beta.

[Image] Notion synthesis doc — findings grouped by persona, with the public beta fix/add/leave list

Across those sessions, users rated the experience an average of 8.2/10, even while encountering beta bugs and performance issues. That told us something important: the core interaction model was approachable. People could open Fabra and start designing without needing to learn traditional 3D software first.

But as they went deeper, a second pattern appeared. Users began asking for more control: measurements and tech packs, garment customisation, bulk edits, better layer management, keyboard shortcuts, accurate colour and material rendering, production-ready exports.

Later rounds of interviews and surveys fed a running insights log and periodic UX Learnings Reports to Gloria and the team — what we’d heard, what the data backed up, and what it meant for the roadmap.

The problem had shifted. We no longer needed to prove that Fabra could be easy. We needed to prove that easy didn’t mean limited.

04 — The key insight

Beginners and professionals didn’t need different products. They needed different levels of the same product.

New founders and non-technical creatives wanted to start with templates, presets and familiar interactions.

Professional designers wanted the same simplicity, but expected more precision as they worked: measurements, patterns, specifications and production controls.

That changed the way I thought about the product. Instead of exposing every capability upfront, Fabra could grow with the user. Start simple. Reveal depth when it becomes useful.

That became the design principle I set for the product, and the one I held every decision against:

Simple by default. Powerful on demand.

05 — Rethinking the product structure

One garment, instead of a collection of disconnected files.

The bigger problem wasn’t any individual feature. It was the fragmentation of the fashion workflow. A designer might update a garment in one tool, adjust the pattern somewhere else, then manually update measurements or production documents to match. Every change created more admin.

Gloria and Nick, our CTO, made the structural call: the garment would become the single source of truth.

The Design Editor, Pattern Studio and Tech Pack would not behave like separate tools. They would become different ways of working with the same garment.

Change the design and the production information stays connected. Adjust the pattern and the garment updates with it. Update materials and those details flow into the tech pack.

My job was to make that decision hold in the interface. I designed how the Design Editor, Pattern Studio and Tech Pack presented themselves as views of one garment rather than three tools — the navigation between them, a shared selection and editing model, and how a change made in one surfaced in the others.

It gave Fabra a much clearer product model:

Design → Construct → Specify → Produce

06 — Designing complexity in layers

The interface needed to feel calm, even when the product became more complex.

Users were quick to notice when something felt off. Unclear selection states, inconsistent click versus drag behaviour, panel overlap, lag or confusing zoom controls — small things that quickly knocked confidence. So before adding more capability, I focused on making the fundamentals predictable.

Selection needed to feel obvious. Common actions needed to behave consistently. The canvas needed to stay visually dominant. Advanced tools needed to show up when you needed them, not compete for attention all at once.

To make that hold as the product grew, we built a design system on a shadcn base, extending it with the interaction patterns Fabra needed. Engineers built from it directly, so a pattern decided once stayed consistent across the editor, Pattern Studio, Tech Pack and everything that came after.

The hardest call wasn’t about the UI. Performance wasn’t on the roadmap between private and public beta. Features were. But every session had someone waiting on the canvas, and every wait cost confidence in everything that came after. I kept bringing the recordings and the quotes back to the team until performance jumped the queue. It shipped fixed for public launch.

How the work moved

With three engineers and no design team, the process had to be light and hard to ignore. Every design went through a review with Gloria before it went anywhere near engineering. Then it went out as Linear tickets with design annotations — and, because some engineers go straight to the file and skip the ticket, the same annotations lived on the Figma frames so nothing depended on which door they came through.

Feedback happened in real time, not in rounds. And every Tuesday at all-hands I brought what users had said that week. If an engineer had room, it was often fixed by Friday. If they didn’t, and I thought it mattered, I brought it back the next Tuesday, and the one after, until it was.

The goal was never to hide complexity. It was to reveal it at the right moment.

07 — Giving people a better starting point

A blank canvas is flexible. It’s also intimidating.

One of the biggest lessons from beta was that accessibility isn’t just about simplifying UI. It’s also about giving people somewhere useful to start.

So instead of forcing every designer into a blank canvas, I designed the entry points around base products, templates, presets and reusable assets.

A designer could start with an existing garment, understand how it was constructed, customise it and gradually move deeper into Fabra’s professional tools.

This created a natural learning loop:

Start with something familiar. Customise it. Understand how it works. Create independently.

The product became part tool, part learning environment. Without turning onboarding into a course.

08 — From video to paying designer

Designing the funnel, not just the product.

As Fabra moved from beta to public launch, the problem changed. We weren’t only designing a product anymore. We were designing a business around it.

Most designers found Fabra through video. So the funnel started long before the canvas:

Video → Landing page → Design tool → Share → Sign-up → Paid plan

I owned the experience at each step. Landing pages built to be tested, so the growth lead and I could run variants without a redesign. Onboarding that got people to a first garment fast. Sharing that made a finished design worth showing. Activation moments that matched why people had turned up in the first place.

Then the same principles carried into billing. Our CGO set the pricing tiers. I designed the flows around them — plan selection, upgrade, billing settings — and QA’d them through to release so they shipped the way they were designed.

Free users needed enough freedom to understand Fabra’s value before being asked to pay. Starter and Pro plans needed to introduce meaningful capability rather than arbitrary restrictions. For AI features, we introduced a credit model so experimentation had a visible cost without forcing designers to make a purchasing decision every time they wanted to try an idea.

The goal was simple: make every step from curious to paying feel so natural that designers would tell other designers about it.

09 — Deciding what shipped

With three engineers, every launch decision was a trade-off.

The product became more complex over the year and so did the questions. Early beta users asked how things worked. As the experience matured, they started asking for more. Text on garments. Custom avatar measurements. Advanced layer controls.

We heard all of it and captured all of it. Then, with Gloria, I sorted every request against the funnel: did it block someone creating a first design, did it block them going deeper into a design they’d already started, or did it block them getting a garment out to production?

Anything in the first two groups was in scope for launch. Anything that made an already-good garment slightly better could wait, however loudly it was asked for.

The goal wasn’t to ship everything. It was to ship the right things first and build something worth adding to. What’s missing is already mapped. It’s a matter of when, not if.

10 — The result

A complete fashion studio, running in the browser.

Fabra evolved from a private beta into an end-to-end platform spanning 3D Design, Pattern Making, Tech Packs, Range Planning, Assets, Print, AI, Onboarding and Billing.

My role evolved with it. In V1, I was deep in the product: the 3D canvas, pattern tools, interaction design, the design system holding it all together. In V2, the work shifted toward getting designers through the door — marketing site, onboarding, monetisation. The full journey from first impression to first paid plan.

Since public beta in November 2025

232,000monthly visitors

17,600sign-ups — a 7.6% visitor-to-sign-up rate

48,400designs created — around 2.75 per designer

34 minmedian time from sign-up to first design

89.1%day 1–7 retention among power users

20.4NPS, from a beta product with known performance issues

The retention numbers are where the design thesis shows up. Power users — about 10% of the active base — come back in weeks two to four and keep coming back in the months after. The people who go deep, stay. Simple by default got them in; powerful on demand kept them.

The same data is honest about the gap. Day 1–7 retention for everyone else is 14.9%. First-step adoption is strong: most people who sign up create a design. What happens after that first design is the next problem to solve.

What designers told us

“This is an amazing program! I’m so happy I found it. I just wish I could change the measurements of the bases specifically to my liking. But something is better than nothing. This is wonderful even without that.”

“What I found surprisingly good was how simple and intuitive the platform was, even for beginners. One improvement I would suggest is providing more customisation options and more guidance for users who are still developing their projects.”

Both say the same thing the data does. Easy to start. Wanting more depth. Designers are opening a browser tab and building real garments, with no software setup and no sales call needed — and then asking what’s next.

11 — What I learned

Simplifying a professional tool isn’t about removing complexity. It’s about deciding when people need to see it.

Complex products don’t become approachable by stripping away functionality. They become approachable by being honest about sequence. What do you need first? What comes next? What can wait?

Designers didn’t need fewer capabilities. They needed them to show up at the right time. That shaped everything. The blank canvas. The templates. The way tools appear as you go deeper. The credit model. The onboarding flow.

Keep the first action obvious. Keep the next action contextual. Keep the full power within reach.

We got the first part right. Thirty-four minutes to a first garment, and most people who sign up make one.

What I’d do differently is design the second session as deliberately as the first. We spent a year making the blank canvas less intimidating and much less time on what brings someone back to a garment they’ve already started. The 14.9% says so. If I were starting again, the return visit would be a first-class design problem from private beta, not a finding from the analytics after launch.

If we get that right, a designer could start with their first garment and still find Fabra useful years later. That still feels worth building.
