Designing Fabra, a professional 3D fashion tool for the browser.

Fabra is a browser-based 3D fashion design platform built to make professional garment creation accessible to designers without traditional 3D expertise.

RoleSenior Product Designer

OwnershipSole product designer across 0→1 product, research, marketing site and launch. Evolved and scaled the existing design system as the product’s surface area grew.

Team3 Engineers · 1 Product Manager · 1 Product-Led Growth

TimelineApr 2025 — Private Beta · Nov 2025 — Public Beta · 2026 — Public Launch & Monetisation

01 — The challenge

Fashion ideas move fast. Getting them into production is slower and messier than it should be.

For most designers, the idea is the easy part. What comes after is harder: turning that idea into something a factory can actually make. In practice that means bouncing between sketches, Illustrator files, spreadsheets, 3D tools, tech packs and physical samples, and hoping nothing gets lost along the way.

The 3D tools that already exist are genuinely powerful, but they’re built for people who’ve trained on them for years. Everyone else hits a wall almost immediately.

Fabra’s bet was that professional 3D fashion design could work in a browser without turning into a toy. That meant solving two things at once: make it simple enough that someone could open it and start, and keep it capable enough to survive a real production workflow.

02 — The early signal

People picked up Fabra fast. The real question was what happened once they got past the first five minutes.

During private beta I ran 30 usability sessions with fashion designers, founders, print designers and other creative professionals, trying to understand where Fabra actually fit into how they already worked. The average rating came out to 8.2/10, higher than I expected given the bugs and rough edges you’d expect from an early beta.

That told me the core interaction model was working. People could open Fabra and start designing without first learning traditional 3D software. But the longer they spent in it, the more specific their requests got:

  • Measurements and tech packs
  • More garment customisation
  • Bulk editing
  • Better layer management
  • Keyboard shortcuts
  • More accurate colour and material rendering
  • Production-ready exports

So the question changed. Getting in the door wasn't the hard part anymore. Turning what someone made into something they could actually use, ship, or hand to a manufacturer was.

03 — Where simplicity broke down

Approachable didn't always mean production-ready.

That mattered more than I expected.

That gap showed up immediately. In every single interview, without exception, the same three things came up: the ability to customise a garment, add a tech pack and export something production-ready. It didn't matter whether I was talking to a print designer, a brand founder or a hobbyist, the ask was identical.

Fabra could get someone designing in minutes. Getting that design out the door and into production was still a different problem entirely.

The single most requested feature, ahead of everything else, was product customisation: adjusting length, measurements and body shape on an existing garment rather than starting from a fixed template.

We weren't monetising yet at this point, so I used these calls to test willingness to pay instead. Most users told me they'd pay a premium if Fabra could meet these production needs specifically, tech packs and customisation, not just polish on the editor itself. That was the clearest signal I had: people didn't just find Fabra easy, they saw it as something that could genuinely replace part of their production pipeline, if we built the parts that made that real.

One recurring debate with engineering was how our panels should scale as the product became more complex. Early versions relied entirely on the left panel. A one-size-fits-all approach for finding, applying and editing content. We experimented with making this even faster through one-click apply, similar to Canva, where selecting an asset adds it directly to the canvas.

But I wasn’t convinced the model would hold up as Fabra gained more advanced controls. My hypothesis was that discovery and configuration were fundamentally different tasks and should have distinct spaces. Figma provided a useful reference: the left side supports browsing and adding, while the right becomes contextual to whatever is selected.

I proposed the same underlying model for Fabra. The left for search, upload and discovery, and the right for granular configuration.

Engineering wasn't convinced, so rather than relying on instinct, I put the idea to the test with 25 users.

Most participants quickly understood the left-for-discovery, right-for-configuration model, including people with little experience using tools like Figma or Canva.

That gave us confidence that we weren’t simply relying on familiarity with another product. The separation itself created a clearer mental model, while giving Fabra an interface that could scale as more professional controls were introduced.

04 — The key insight

Beginners and professionals seemed to want very different things from Fabra.

But I didn’t think they needed different products. They needed different depths of the same one.

New founders and non-technical creatives wanted an easy place to start, with templates, presets and familiar interactions.

Professional designers wanted that same simplicity, but expected more precision as they went deeper, with patterns, measurements, specifications and production controls.

The challenge was designing for both without overwhelming one or limiting the other.

The fix wasn’t a beginner mode or a pro mode. It was a product that could grow with whoever was using it:

Simple by default. Powerful on demand.

05 — Rethinking the product structure

The bigger problem wasn’t any one feature. It was fragmentation.

A designer might visualise a garment in one tool, adjust the pattern somewhere else, track measurements in a spreadsheet, then build production docs separately. Change the garment and everything downstream needs to be checked and updated by hand.

So we made a structural call: the garment itself would become the single source of truth.

Instead of treating the Design Editor, Pattern Studio and Tech Pack as separate products, we designed them as different ways of working with the same garment.

A user can change the design and the production data stays connected. Adjust the pattern and the garment updates with it. Update a material and it flows into the tech pack.

Design → Construct → Specify → Produce
3D Editor → Pattern Studio → Tech Pack → Export

One garment, one source of truth, the whole way through. This gave us a much clearer model to build around. As Fabra grew, we could ask a simpler question of every new capability: where does it belong in the garment’s journey from idea to production?

06 — Designing complexity in layers

A professional tool accumulates controls whether you want it to or not.

The real question was when those controls earned a user’s attention.

The canvas stayed at the centre of the experience. Tools and properties became contextual instead of permanent, showing up based on what someone was doing or had selected rather than sitting on screen all the time.

As the product grew, that same thinking had to extend beyond the editor into the design system itself.

We brought in an external designer for a month to establish the design system based on Fabra’s brand, while I focused on UX strategy. From there, I took ownership of evolving and extending the system as Fabra grew from one editor into eight interconnected tools.

That meant introducing new components and interaction patterns as new problems emerged, while keeping the underlying experience consistent. Eight tools could do very different things without feeling like eight different products stitched together.

That came with real trade-offs. Some controls became less visible. Some workflows picked up an extra step. Not everything got to live permanently on the canvas. But the alternative was an interface where every feature fought for the same attention at once — and that was worse.

The goal was never to hide complexity. It was to show it at the moment it actually became useful.

07 — Giving people a better starting point

A blank canvas is flexible. It’s also a little intimidating if you don’t already know what you’re doing.

One of the bigger lessons from beta was that accessibility isn’t only about simplifying the UI. It’s also about giving people somewhere useful to start.

A blank canvas works well for an experienced designer, but it assumes a new user already understands what Fabra can do and how a garment gets built.

Instead of taking that flexibility away, we built more ways into the product: base products, templates, presets and reusable assets, with Blank Canvas still there for anyone who wanted full control from the first click.

Someone could start with an existing garment, see how it was put together, customise it, then gradually work their way into the more professional tools. Multiple ways in, with one product underneath.

That created a learning loop that worked in our favour:

Start with something familiar → Customise it → Understand how it works → Create independently

Fabra became part tool, part learning environment, without turning onboarding into a course.

08 — Deciding what not to build

The roadmap was always going to be bigger than three engineers and one designer could actually ship.

Research kept surfacing good ideas: custom avatars, advanced measurements, grading, text tools, collaboration, richer exports, better layers and deeper garment customisation. Most deserved to exist eventually. We just couldn’t build them all at once.

So prioritisation became part of the design process, not something that happened after it.

Every request was weighed against the same four things: how often it came up in research, how critical it was to completing the Design → Construct → Specify → Produce loop, its strategic value to Fabra, and what it would cost the team to build properly.

Not every good idea was the next idea. The question was never, “Would this make Fabra better?” Almost everything would have. The question that mattered was:

“What unlocks the next most important part of the workflow?”

That helped protect the core experience while the product kept expanding around it.

09 — Making UX part of the product strategy

UX should shape the roadmap, not just respond to it.

I presented UX OKRs and brought usability findings and recurring friction into planning conversations rather than leaving them inside design reviews.

When issues like the click-versus-drag inconsistency and keyboard shortcuts kept showing up in research, they had to compete for priority against new feature requests. That meant making the case with evidence, not just design opinion.

Over time, this helped make UX a clearer part of how we talked about product priorities across the team, rather than a collection of individual design fixes.

With three engineers and an ambitious roadmap, not everything could be addressed at once. One of the most challenging parts of design was making the case for what mattered now, what could wait and where improving the experience would create the most value for both users and the business.

10 — From product to business

Turning a product into a business

The next challenge was growth: designing the path to monetisation while working towards 10k monthly active users.

The hard part was keeping the same sense of experimentation that made Fabra approachable in the first place. Free users needed enough room to actually feel the value before we asked them to pay. Starter and Pro needed to unlock capability that felt meaningful, not like arbitrary walls we’d put up.

For AI features specifically, we introduced credits so the cost of generation stayed visible and understandable without forcing a purchase decision every time someone wanted to try an idea.

Show value first. Introduce commitment when the value is already clear.

The same logic applied everywhere else. The goal was for the whole path, from curious visitor to first design to returning designer to paying customer, to feel like one experience instead of a product with a business bolted onto it.

11 — The result

What started as one editor became eight tools

Fabra grew from a private beta into a full platform spanning 3D Design, Pattern Making, Tech Packs, Range Planning, Assets, Repeat Print Maker, AI and Billing.

My role grew with it. In V1, I was deep in the core experience: the 3D canvas, pattern tools, interaction models and the design system holding it together. Moving into V2 and monetisation, my scope expanded across the whole customer journey: marketing, acquisition, onboarding, activation, pricing and billing.

I went from designing how someone edits a garment to designing how they find Fabra, understand it, start creating and eventually decide to pay for it.

As of September 2026, Fabra sees roughly

232,000monthly visitors

17,600sign-ups

48,400designs created

Within that broader growth, I owned the design of key conversion and activation moments: how people entered the product, the dashboard experience and the journey to creating their first design.

But one of the more interesting signals was how the questions from designers changed. Early beta feedback was mostly about understanding how the product worked. As Fabra matured, designers started asking how far they could take it: custom avatar measurements, advanced layer controls, text on garments and deeper production functionality.

The conversation had shifted from “How do I use this?” to “Can I do this too?” Those questions became signals for where the product could go next.

With a small engineering team, every launch was a trade-off. The goal was never to ship everything. It was to ship the right things first, watch how people actually used them and build from there.

12 — What I learned

Simplifying a professional tool isn’t about stripping out complexity. It’s about deciding when someone is ready to see it.

That ended up being the thread running through almost every decision I made on Fabra.

Give someone too much too early and the product becomes intimidating. Hide too much and it stops being useful as they become more capable. The challenge is designing the space between those two things.

Keep the first action obvious. Keep the next action contextual. Keep the full power within reach.

Get that right, and someone can build their first garment without knowing anything about 3D, while still finding new depth in the product as their skills and needs grow.
