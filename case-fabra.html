Designing Fabra, a professional 3D fashion tool for the browser.

Fabra is a browser-based 3D fashion design platform built to make professional garment creation accessible to designers without traditional 3D expertise.

Senior Product (UX) Designer | Mar 2025 - Continue

RoleSenior Product Designer

OwnershipSole product designer across 0→1 product, research, marketing site and launch. Evolved and scaled the existing design system as the product’s surface area grew.

Team3 Engineers · 1 Product Manager · 1 Product-Led Growth

TimelineApr 2025 — Private Beta
Nov 2025 — Public Beta
Aug 2026 — Public Launch & Monetisation

01 — The challenge

Fashion ideas move fast. Getting them into production is slower and messier than it should be.

For most designers, the idea is the easy part. What comes after is harder: turning that idea into something a factory can actually make. In practice that means bouncing between sketches, Illustrator files, spreadsheets, 3D tools, tech packs and physical samples, and hoping nothing gets lost along the way.

The 3D tools that already exist are genuinely powerful, but they’re built for people who’ve trained on them for years. Everyone else hits a wall almost immediately.

Fabra’s bet was that professional 3D fashion design could work in a browser without turning into a toy. That meant solving two things at once: make it simple enough that someone could open it and start, and keep it capable enough to survive a real production workflow.

02 — The early signal

People picked up Fabra fast. The real question was what happened once they got past the first five minutes.

During private beta I ran 30 usability sessions with fashion designers, founders, print designers and other creative professionals, trying to understand where Fabra actually fit into how they already worked. The average rating came out to 8.2/10, higher than I expected given the bugs and rough edges you’d expect from an early beta.

That told me the core interaction model was working. People could open Fabra and start designing without first learning traditional 3D software. But the longer they spent in it, the more specific their requests got:

  • Measurements and tech packs
  • More garment customisation
  • Bulk editing
  • Better layer management
  • Keyboard shortcuts
  • More accurate colour and material rendering
  • Production-ready exports

So the question changed. Getting in the door wasn't the hard part anymore. Turning what someone made into something they could actually use, ship, or hand to a manufacturer was.

03 — Where simplicity broke down

Approachable didn't always mean production-ready.

That mattered more than I expected.

That gap showed up immediately. In every single interview, without exception, the same three things came up: the ability to customise a garment, add a tech pack and export something production-ready. It didn't matter whether I was talking to a print designer, a brand founder or a hobbyist, the ask was identical.

Fabra could get someone designing in minutes. Getting that design out the door and into production was still a different problem entirely.

The single most requested feature, ahead of everything else, was product customisation: adjusting length, measurements and body shape on an existing garment rather than starting from a fixed template.

We weren't monetising yet, so I used these calls to test willingness to pay instead. Most users told me they'd pay a premium if Fabra could meet these production needs specifically, tech packs and customisation, not just polish on the editor. People didn't just find Fabra easy. They saw it as something that could genuinely replace part of their production pipeline, provided we actually built the parts that made that true.

One recurring debate with engineering was how our panels should scale as Fabra got more complex. Early on, everything, search, apply, configure, lived in one left panel, the same one-click logic as Canva. It made sense when there wasn't much to configure yet.

But I wasn’t convinced the model would hold up as Fabra gained more advanced controls. My hypothesis was that discovery and configuration were fundamentally different tasks and should have distinct spaces. Figma provided a useful reference: the left side supports browsing and adding, while the right becomes contextual to whatever is selected.

I proposed the same underlying model for Fabra. The left for search, upload and discovery, and the right for granular configuration.

There was uncertainty around whether the added complexity was worth it, so I tested the idea with 25 users.

Most participants quickly understood the left-for-discovery, right-for-configuration model, including people with little experience using tools like Figma or Canva.

That gave us confidence that we weren’t simply relying on familiarity with another product. The separation itself created a clearer mental model, while giving Fabra an interface that could scale as more professional controls were introduced.

04 — The key insight

Beginners and professionals seemed to want very different things from Fabra.

I didn’t think they needed different products. They needed different depths of the same one.

New founders and non-technical creatives wanted an easy place to start, with templates, presets and familiar interactions.

Professional designers wanted that same simplicity, but expected more precision as they went deeper, with patterns, measurements, specifications and production controls.

The challenge was designing for both without overwhelming one or limiting the other.

The fix wasn’t a beginner mode or a pro mode. It was a product that could grow with whoever was using it:

Simple by default. Powerful on demand.

05 — Rethinking the product structure

The bigger problem wasn’t any one feature. It was fragmentation.

A designer might visualise a garment in one tool, adjust the pattern in another, track measurements in a spreadsheet, then build production docs separately. Every change creates more work downstream, with details needing to be checked and updated by hand.

So we made a structural decision: the garment itself would become the single source of truth.

Rather than treating the Design Editor, Pattern Studio and Tech Pack as separate products, we designed them as connected ways of working with the same garment.

Now, when a designer makes a change, they don’t have to go back and update everything else. Adjust the pattern and the garment updates with it. Swap a material and it’s already reflected in the tech pack.

Design → Construct → Specify → Produce
3D Editor → Pattern Studio → Tech Pack → Export

One garment, one source of truth, the whole way through.

That gave us a much clearer foundation to build from. As Fabra grew, every new capability had to answer the same question: where does this fit in the journey from idea to production?

06 — Designing complexity in layers

As Fabra became more capable, the interface became more complex.

The real question was when those controls earned a user’s attention.

The canvas stayed at the centre of the experience. Tools and properties became contextual instead of permanent, showing up based on what someone was doing or had selected rather than sitting on screen all the time.

As the product grew, that same thinking had to extend beyond the editor into the design system itself.

We brought in an external designer for a month to establish the design system based on Fabra’s brand, while I focused on UX strategy. From there, I took ownership of evolving and extending the system as Fabra grew from one editor into eight interconnected tools.

As new problems emerged, I introduced new components and interaction patterns while keeping the experience consistent. Each tool could do something different without feeling like a different product.

That came with trade-offs. Some controls became less visible. Some workflows picked up an extra step. Not everything could live permanently on the canvas. The goal was to keep complexity from becoming clutter, so new features didn’t all compete for attention at once.

The goal wasn’t to hide complexity. It was to surface it when it became useful.

07 — Giving people a better starting point

A blank canvas gives you complete freedom. It also assumes you know where to start.

One of the bigger lessons from beta was that accessibility isn’t only about simplifying the UI. It’s also about giving people somewhere useful to start.

A blank canvas is great if you’re an experienced designer. For someone new, it assumes you already know what Fabra can do, how a garment gets built and where to begin.

So instead of taking that flexibility away, we built more ways into the product: base products, templates, presets and reusable assets. Blank Canvas was still there for anyone who wanted full control from the first click.

Someone could start with an existing garment, see how it was put together, customise it, then gradually work their way into the more professional tools. Multiple ways in, with one product underneath.

That created a learning loop that worked in our favour:

Start with something familiar → Customise it → Understand how it works → Create independently

Fabra became part tool, part learning environment, without turning onboarding into a course.

08 — Deciding what not to build

The roadmap was always going to be bigger than three engineers and one designer could actually ship.

Research kept surfacing good ideas: custom avatars, advanced measurements, grading, text tools, collaboration, richer exports, better layers and deeper garment customisation. Most deserved to exist eventually. We just couldn’t build them all at once.

So figuring out what not to build became just as important as figuring out what to build.

Every request came back to the same four things: how often we heard it in research, where it sat in the Design → Construct → Specify → Produce loop, what it could unlock for Fabra, and how much work it would take to build well.

The tricky part was that most of the ideas were good ideas. They would make Fabra better. They just couldn’t all come next.

So the question became:

““What unlocks the next most important part of the workflow?””

That gave us a way to keep making progress without needing to solve everything at once.

09 — Making UX part of the product strategy

UX should shape the roadmap, not just respond to it.

I started bringing UX OKRs, usability findings and recurring friction into planning conversations, rather than letting them live inside design reviews.

When things like click-versus-drag inconsistencies and missing keyboard shortcuts kept coming up in research, they were competing for the same time as new features. I needed to show why fixing them mattered, using what we were hearing and seeing from users rather than relying on design opinion alone.

Over time, UX became a bigger part of how we decided what to work on, not just something we improved once a feature reached design.

With three engineers and a long list of things we wanted to build, we couldn’t fix everything at once. The harder question was deciding what mattered now, what could wait, and where better UX could make the biggest difference for users and for Fabra.

10 — From product to business

Designing for monetisation

The next challenge was growth: finding a path to monetisation while working towards 10k monthly editor users.

Monthly editor users = Users who enter the playground editor or the logged in editor/dashboard

With the launch of V2 in August 2026, Fabra was moving into monetisation. The tricky part was keeping the sense of experimentation that made the product approachable in the first place. Free users still needed enough room to make something, explore what Fabra could do for them and have a reason to come back.

At the same time, Starter and Pro needed to offer something genuinely useful, so upgrading felt like a natural next step rather than a barrier to getting started.

We wanted free users to have enough space to discover what made Fabra useful. They could make something, explore what the product could do for them and have a reason to come back. By the time they reached the limits of the free plan, upgrading should feel like a natural next step.

Show value first. Introduce commitment when the value is already clear.

And it wasn’t just about where we put the paywall. A curious visitor, someone making their first design, a returning designer and eventually a paying customer weren’t separate experiences. Each step needed to naturally lead into the next, so monetisation felt like part of the product rather than something layered on top.

11 — The result

What started as one editor became eight tools

Fabra grew from a private beta into a full platform spanning 3D Design, Pattern Making, Tech Packs, Range Planning, Assets, Repeat Print Maker, AI and Billing.

As the product grew, so did the scope of what I was designing. In V1, I was deep in the core experience: the 3D canvas, pattern tools, interaction models and the design system holding it together. By V2 and monetisation, that had expanded across the entire customer journey, from marketing and acquisition through to onboarding, activation, pricing and billing.

I was now designing beyond the editor, across how people discovered Fabra, started using it and eventually upgraded.

As of September 2026, Fabra sees roughly

232,000monthly visitors

17,600sign-ups

48,400designs created

As more people came into the product, I focused on some of the moments that shaped whether they actually got started: how they entered Fabra, what they saw when they arrived and how they got to their first design.

Over time, we started to notice a shift in what designers were asking for. Early beta feedback was mostly about understanding how the product worked. As Fabra matured, the questions became more ambitious: custom avatar measurements, advanced layer controls, text on garments and deeper production functionality.

The conversation had shifted from “How do I use this?” to “Can I do this too?”

It was a good signal that people were starting to see more possibilities in the product.

For me, that shift said a lot about how far the product had come. People were no longer just learning how to use Fabra. They were starting to imagine what else they could do with it.

There’s still plenty we want to build, but that’s a pretty good place for the next chapter to start.

12 — What I learned

Simplifying a professional tool isn’t about stripping out complexity. It’s about deciding when someone is ready to see it.

That idea came up again and again while designing Fabra.

Show someone too much too early and the product becomes intimidating. Hide too much and it becomes limiting as they get more confident. The challenge was finding the right balance.

Keep the first action obvious. Keep the next action contextual. Keep the full power within reach.

For Fabra, that meant someone could create their first garment without knowing anything about 3D, then discover more of the product as their skills and needs grew.
